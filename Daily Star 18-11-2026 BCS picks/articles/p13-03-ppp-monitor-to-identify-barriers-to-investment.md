# PPP Monitor to identify barriers to investment

_STAR BUSINESS REPORT — Star Business — PDF p13, p15_

The Asian Development Bank (ADB) and the Invest Bangladesh Authority yesterday launched a tool to assess Bangladesh’s public-private partnership (PPP) environment and identify barriers to private investment in infrastructure.

The PPP Monitor will help policymakers, development banks and investors understand the country’s investment climate by examining regulations, institutional capacity, project pipelines, financing arrangements and sector-specific challenges.

Finance and Planning Minister Amir Khosru Mahmud Chowdhury and Invest Bangladesh Chairman Ashik Chowdhury jointly launched the tool at a programme at the InterContinental Dhaka.

The tool serves two purposes: helping the government identify policy gaps and allowing investors to assess risks before committing funds. It uses more than 500 qualitative and quantitative indicators to evaluate a country’s PPP environment, according to an ADB presentation.

Developed for around 12 countries, including Bangladesh, the monitor is expected to cover more countries in the future.

Its assessment covers three levels: national, sectoral and local government. At the national level, it examines policies, regulations, project approval processes, government support and investment barriers.

At the sectoral level, it looks at project pipelines, procurement, financing and risk-sharing arrangements. The local government assessment focuses on governance, infrastructure planning and municipal finances.

The tool covers eight infrastructure sectors: roads, railways, ports, airports, energy, water and wastewater, information and communication technology, and social infrastructure.

### PRIVATE INVESTMENT KEY TO MEETING INFRASTRUCTURE NEEDS

Speaking at the launch, Khosru said PPPs would play an important role in the country’s economic development as the government aims to build a $1 trillion economy by 2034.

“Public resources alone cannot meet our infrastructure needs. We must mobilise private capital, technology and expertise on a much larger scale,” he said.

He said private sector-led growth was a founding principle of the government’s political philosophy, making PPPs an important way to attract investment and improve public services.

However, he cautioned that not every project was suitable for a PPP and that private financing should not be treated as free money.

“It is ultimately repaid through project revenues, user charges or government payments. Guarantees and viability gap financing must therefore be carefully assessed and transparently managed,” he said.

Khosru said the PPP Monitor would help identify weaknesses in the country’s policy, institutional and regulatory frameworks.

“Its real value will lie in action: better project preparation, predictable approvals, transparent procurement and effective contract management,” he said.

He outlined three priorities: developing a pipeline of bankable projects, mobilising private capital, and strengthening institutional capacity and accountability.

He also stressed the need to complete feasibility studies, ensure land availability and utility connections, and settle risk-sharing arrangements before presenting projects to investors.

Khosru urged the ADB to work with Invest Bangladesh and relevant ministries to prepare a list of priority projects and attract credible investors.

Takeo Koike, head of ADB’s Office of Markets Development and Public- Private Partnership, said Bangladesh had a major opportunity to use private sector expertise, innovation and capital to develop sustainable infrastructure and improve public services.

A strong policy and regulatory framework, capable institutions, transparent processes and well-prepared projects were essential to building a successful PPP market, he said.
