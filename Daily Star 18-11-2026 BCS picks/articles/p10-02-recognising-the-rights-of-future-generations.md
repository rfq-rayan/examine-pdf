*LAW OPINION*

# Recognising the rights of future generations

_TAPOSHI RABEYA — Law & Our Rights — PDF p10_

![](../images/p10_01.jpg)

The Constitution of Bangladesh does not explicitly recognise the rights of future generations but places them in Part II of the Constitution, which is judicially non-enforceable. Although in 2025, the Constitution Reform Commission of Bangladesh proposed to include the rights of future generations as fundamental rights under the Constitution, it was eventually not incorporated into the Constitution.

However, a progressive interpretation of the Constitution would show that the fundamental rights enshrined in the Constitution are also applicable to future generations, as human rights law does not limit itself to present generation, and the Constitution of Bangladesh neither provides for any temporal constraint on the fundamental rights nor expressly restricts such rights only to the present generation. But this raises the question: if future generations are entitled to the fundamental rights under the Constitution, how will those rights be enforced when they are violated?

The Appellate Division of the Supreme Court of Bangladesh, in the case of Dr. Mohiuddin Farooque v Bangladesh (1996), rejected Dr. Farooque’s submission that the Bangladesh Environmental Lawyers Association represents not only the present generation but also the generation yet unborn. Dr. Farooque’s claim was based on the Philippines’ Oposa doctrine from the case Juan Antonio Oposa and others v Hon’ble Fulgencio S. Factoran, where the plaintiff minors also represented the future generations, invoking the twin concepts of ‘intergenerational responsibility’ and ‘intergenerational justice’. The Appellate Division’s main rationale for rejecting Dr Farooque’s argument was that Bangladesh’s Constitution did not contain any analogous provision.

Nevertheless, what the Philippine court actually held in the judgment while granting the locus standi was, ‘Their personality to sue on behalf of the succeeding generations can only be based on the concept of intergenerational responsibility insofar as the right to a balanced and healthful ecology is concerned.’ The provision based on which the Philippine court granted minors standing was section 16 under the State Policy part of the Philippine Constitution, which does not explicitly mention the rights of the future generation. It says, ‘The State shall protect and advance the right of the people to a balanced and healthful ecology in accord with the rhythm and harmony of nature,’ and the Court expounded the term ‘rhythm and harmony of nature’ with reference to the policy under section 1, Title XIV, Environment and Natural Resources, of the Administrative Code of 1987. So, it was not the Philippine Constitution that explicitly entitled fundamental rights to future generations; rather, it was the court that liberalised the standing with progressive interpretation.

Moreover, as discussed earlier, the 15th Amendment of the Constitution of Bangladesh incorporated protection of the natural resources, biodiversity, wetlands, forests and wildlife for the present and future citizens through the insertion of Article 18A in 2011. Would the Court have allowed the association to represent the future generation if Article 18A was present at that time? One may argue that Article 18A under Part II of the Constitution is a fundamental principle of state policy, but so is the nature of Section 16 of the Philippines’ Constitution, and judicial precedents in Bangladesh show both negative and positive enforcement of the rights enshrined under Part II.

Furthermore, in the landmark Urgenda case in the Netherlands, the Hague District Court allowed Urgenda’s standing to act on behalf of the current and future generations. When the State challenged it, the Hague Court of Appeal held that Urgenda’s claim was admissible insofar as Urgenda acted on behalf of the interests of the current generation of Dutch nationals and individuals within its jurisdiction, and thus it believed that it did not have to consider the questions raised by the State about future generations. Our court could have taken this approach instead of outright rejecting the idea that an association can represent future generations. Our court could also have used the interpretative tools dubio pro homine and dubio pro natura, according to which the most beneficial interpretation and application of norms for individuals and nature, respectively, ought to guide the decision.

It is evident that while Bangladesh contains a weak de jure provision regarding the rights of future generations, the de facto protection is even more absent. To protect the rights of future generations, we need to make our de jure provisions stronger and focus on de facto enforcement at the same time.
