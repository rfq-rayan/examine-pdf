# Another climate summit, the same unanswered questions

_Editorial — PDF p6_

![](../images/p06_01.jpg)

Every year, curtains are raised and climate delegates walk onto the huge stage: heads of states deliver inspirational speeches, new initiatives are announced, and the final plenary wraps up with claps and praises for an agreement that very few believe would be fully delivered. The 31st United Nations Climate Change Conference, better known as COP31, is in progress in Antalya, Türkiye, and once again some of us are left wondering who will actually deliver on their promises. Are we watching diplomacy or merely a political performance?

In 2021, celebrated climate activist Greta Thunberg said world leaders’ promises were “blah, blah, blah” and the COP was a PR stunt with beautiful speeches and fancy targets. This week, she attended COP31, her first climate conference since COP26 in 2021, and called the event hypocritical. In 2021, she had said that nature and physics don’t play along with the theatrics of climate talks. Five years on, the show is bigger, and science has not let up.

The COP31 presidency is held by Türkiye, but Australia is leading the negotiations. This unusual arrangement ended months of deadlock over who would host the event, but it also raised confusion as to who is actually in charge. In September this year, Ankara said the final say was up to the COP31 presidency. This means it is even less clear as to who will be held accountable for this COP.

Last year, COP30 was known as the “COP of Truth”. However, there was no roadmap for fossil fuel phase-out, and countries could not reach an agreement on including transitioning away from fossil fuels and ending deforestation in the official text. The Brazilian presidency has pledged to work on voluntary roadmaps outside the UN system. The main positive was that there was a call to triple adaptation finance by 2035. While this is moving forward after years of stalled negotiations, many vulnerable nations wanted an earlier deadline of 2030. Developed countries mostly reiterated that they would follow the Baku pathway and deliver at least $300 billion per year by 2035.

However, climate finance inflow has fallen significantly in 2026. Before COP31 began, a senior EU official said there would be no political will for increased funding pledges. The 2035 deadline means that we can simply forget about keeping our promises of annual contributions and deliver on them later.

The United States, one of the big polluters, presents the biggest example of broken promises. The country formally left the Paris Agreement in January this year, and did not send an official delegation to COP for the second year running. It is on track to be the first country to leave the UN climate convention as well. The country has not faced any consequences. Several governors, mayors, and state and local leaders are attending COP31 in place of the federal government. This shows that a national government can simply walk away from their responsibilities.

The most honest speech this year may have come from Nepal’s Prime Minister Balendra Shah—not at COP31, but at the UN General Assembly in September. He focused on the devastating Bhotekoshi-Trishuli floods in his country and highlighted that climate change does not care about borders. He also stressed that in a world where weapons can be delivered anywhere in 72 hours, we cannot leave farmers helpless.

This is the real message behind COP this year and Bangladesh can relate. For farmers in Satkhira, fishermen in Cox’s Bazar, and families losing land to river erosion, the success of COPs means money will be delivered on time as grants to counter the impact of the climate crisis. Our negotiators attending the conference should boldly ask: who will pay, when will they pay, and what will happen to those who don’t pay on time?

At home, we will lose money if adaptation funds are lost in delays, confusion, and poor targeting. We also weaken our moral argument abroad every time rivers are grabbed or wetlands filled.

Treaties are judged on how well they are delivered. COP31 will be judged the same way. We may hear the usual jargon of “progress” and “a missed opportunity” when the final text is agreed upon at the end of the conference. The best way to judge it is by looking at how much money will be delivered to those losing their homes and livelihoods and what will happen to the member-states who don’t deliver. Until these questions are answered, COP will be exactly what its critics say: the most expensive stage in the world with the planet as our audience.
