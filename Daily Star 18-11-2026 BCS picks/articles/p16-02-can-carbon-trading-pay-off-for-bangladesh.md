# Can carbon trading pay off for Bangladesh?

_M JAMAL UDDIN — Star Business — PDF p16_

![](../images/p16_01.jpg)

Carbon trading has featured prominently at COP31, the UN climate conference taking place in Antalya, Türkiye. Under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015, countries seek transparent international carbon markets.

Bangladesh is also preparing to develop a national framework for carbon trading, Prime Minister Tarique Rahman announced while addressing the High-Level Segment of the conference on November 12.

Carbon trading is a market-based way to cut greenhouse gas emissions. Projects that achieve additional, measurable reductions or removals of one tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent (tCO₂e) may earn credits under recognised standards. Countries and companies buy them to meet targets, including voluntary corporate commitments.

Article 6.2 of the Paris Agreement enables bilateral transactions, while Article 6.4 establishes a UN-supervised crediting mechanism. Corresponding adjustments stop buyers and sellers from counting the same reduction towards their targets.

On November 12, UN Trade and Development (UNCTAD) launched its Global Carbon Market Network to promote cooperation, transparency and investment in developing countries. According to UNCTAD, carbon-pricing mechanisms raised over $107 billion in public revenue in 2025.

### WHY BANGLADESH NEEDS IT?

Bangladesh emits less than 0.5 percent of global greenhouse gases, around 0.4 percent according to the World Bank, yet remains highly vulnerable because of its dense population and low-lying geography.

Cyclones, floods, salinity, erosion and heatwaves threaten farming and food security. The World Bank warns climate change could cut agricultural GDP by up to one-third by 2050. Carbon trading could supplement climate grants.

The prime minister, in his speech at COP31, announced plans for planting 250 million trees in five years, coastal mangrove restoration, 4,000 megawatts of solar power and waste-to-energy projects.

These could generate credits if properly implemented. Prospects span forestry, mangroves, renewables, agriculture and waste. Blue carbon projects can protect coasts and attract finance. Existing protected forests, including the Sundarbans, cannot be claimed as new achievements. Projects must prove additionality, permanence and biodiversity safeguards.

Rice-field water management, alternate wetting and drying, balanced fertiliser, agroforestry, biogas and crop-residue use can cut emissions, lower costs and increase farmers’ incomes.

Bangladesh has about 300 LEED-certified garment factories, but certification alone earns no credits. Additional reductions, credible baselines and international verification are essential. Carbon markets could attract investment to factories and renewable energy.

The EU’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism entered its definitive phase on January 1, 2026, requiring certificates for certain carbon-intensive imports based on production emissions. Exporters need better emissions measurement, energy efficiency and renewable energy.

### CHALLENGES AND POLICY PRIORITIES

Bangladesh lacks the expertise, technology and institutions for reliable measurement, reporting and verification (MRV).

Registration under Verra or Gold Standard is costly. Volatile prices, intermediaries and poor-quality credits add risks. The country needs a national policy, a legal framework and a digital registry, backed by coordinated institutions across environment, forestry, agriculture, power, industry and finance. Internationally aligned MRV and independent verification are vital.

Farmers, small businesses and communities need participation and fair revenue shares. Small projects such as solar irrigation pilots, improved cook stoves, mangrove restoration, rice-water management and waste-methane capture could be bundled to reduce costs.

Climate justice remains paramount, so Bangladesh must seek grants, adaptation finance and technology transfer. The prime minister urged allocating at least half of international climate finance to adaptation and providing grants rather than loans.

Carbon trading, however, does not bring quick profit. Sound policies, scientific measurement, standards, transparency and fair benefit-sharing could link environmental protection with farm incomes, green industry and investment, and advance Bangladesh’s sustainable economy.
